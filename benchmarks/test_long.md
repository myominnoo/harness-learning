# Notes from a Mountain Village

This notebook collects small observations from a quiet mountain village in late autumn. The mornings begin early because the baker works before sunrise, and the smell of warm dough carries down the valley road. Most of the houses keep a garden, even on the steep terraces where the soil is thin and the stones are hard to move. In October the families dry apples in long rows, stack firewood under covered porches, and mend nets that have frayed during the fishing season. The air is cold but clear, and on still evenings you can watch the last light stay on the peaks long after it has left the fields below.

The river behind the village has a simple story if you follow it far enough. Spring snowmelt feeds its upper reaches, and each thaw sends new sediment tumbling over the rocks. Fishermen say that the best trout hide in the deep pools where the current slows after a bend. In summer the water is high and brown, but by September it turns glassy and cold. Nobody swims in it, and the children learn early to respect the depth. A flat stone thrown at the surface can skip across the current for a dozen yards before it sinks, and the old men still compete each August to see who can throw the farthest.

There is a library in the district seat, though it would not pass as one by the standards of a large city. The building was originally a grain warehouse, which explains the high ceilings and the deep windows that look out over the plaza. Three hundred shelves hold most of the collection: agricultural handbooks, almanacs, travel books, and a surprising number of cookbooks. The librarian, a patient woman with ink-stained fingers, keeps a small ledger of late returns and a shelf of stories for the younger children. Visitors from the capital find the place dusty and modest, but the regulars treat it as the center of their week, and the reading room is full before noon on market days.

Winter cooking in the valley follows a rhythm older than memory. The autumn harvest decides what appears on the table for the next four months. Pumpkins are carved into rings and dried on racks. Beans are shucked in the evenings with neighbors, the husks piling into a bucket that empties into the hen coop before dark. The family pot sits on the hearth from early afternoon, and by the time supper is called it has barely stopped bubbling. Nobody consults a recipe. The cook tastes, adds a spoon of salt, a pinch of pepper, and trusts that the same hand which has done it a hundred times will do it again.

The school is a two-room building with a stone chimney between them. Lessons run from nine until midday, and the children walk home along the same muddy track every day, regardless of the weather. The teacher keeps a strict schedule of arithmetic, reading, and writing, but the afternoon is less orderly. Boys practice throwing the wooden ball in the yard, and girls arrange flowers on the windowsill. When the bell rings, the building seems to exhale, and the street fills with running shoes, shouted names, and the occasional scraped knee that is wiped on a coat sleeve and immediately forgotten.

Weather is the one topic that never runs out of words. The farmers have a name for every turn of the sky: the wind that strips the last leaves, the fog that hangs in the valley for three days, the unseasonable frost that arrives with a week of sunshine on its back. They watch the birds, the color of the sunset, and the direction of the clouds, and they make their predictions with the quiet confidence of people who have been wrong before but keep watching anyway. When the forecast from the city contradicts the old signs, the village usually waits a day to see who proves right.
